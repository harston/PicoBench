PicoBench board comparison

Three boards measured with PicoBench: a Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040) clone, a Pico 2 (RP2350A) clone and a WeAct RP2350B Core with PSRAM. The comparison covers CPU, SRAM, flash/XIP in every read mode the chips offer (serial, dual, quad, with and without the command byte, three flash clocks), PSRAM, the Atari cartridge "hot path", peripherals, and how all of it scales with the system clock from 48 to 200 MHz. A last section shows how far each chip goes outside its specification (overclocking run).

The same comparison as Markdown: board-comparison.md.

All timings are internal: CPU and bus cycles counted by the chip itself (SysTick on the M0+, DWT CYCCNT on the M33, mcycle on Hazard3). Electrical timing (rise time, PCB propagation, input thresholds) is not measured. Latencies are reported as p50 / p99.9 / max over the stated samples; "cyc" = clk_sys cycles.

Quad on the RP2350A needs the flash's Quad Enable bit, which is clear on this board. The chip accepts a volatile QE write (50h + 31h), which is lost at power-off. With QE set, the RP2350A reads quad at exactly the WeAct's figures. Without it, quad reads return wrong data, and the boot ROM therefore picks dual. The dual-mode penalty of this board is purely that one bit.

Cost of one flash cache miss

A cache miss fetches one 8-byte line in one SPI frame. On the RP2350 its cost is (SCK cycles of the frame) x clock divider + ~5 cycles. The frame is command + address + mode byte + dummy + 64 data bits, each at its bus width. This model predicts every measured RP2350 figure to within 6 cycles.

The RP2040 hands the word to the CPU once the first 32 data bits are in: 20 SCK cycles x divider + ~13-15 cycles. That gives 53 cycles at divider 2 and 95 at divider 4, the latter measured in the overclocking run.

Random access to a large working set is faster uncached. The dependent-load rows walk a random cycle through every word of the working set once per sample (results_15). 8 KB fits in the 16 KB XIP cache, so every load hits. At 256 KB nearly every load misses, and a cached miss fetches a whole 8-byte line (66 cycles) where an uncached read fetches one word (55). Through the cached alias that costs 66.9 cycles per load, through the uncached alias 53.1, 21 % less. The ratio is the same at every clock (36.0 / 29.1 cycles at 48 MHz with divider 1). For tables much larger than the cache that are read at random, use the uncached alias. The earlier figure of ~3.3 cycles for "256 KB cached" walked only 256 steps (2 KB), which fit in the cache.

The SDK picks the PSRAM divider by clock. It uses divider 1 at 48 and 96 MHz (PSRAM SCK = clk_sys) and divider 2 from 120 MHz up. A single uncached read therefore costs 32 cycles (333 ns) at 96 MHz but 55 cycles (458 ns) at 120 MHz, and streaming is faster at 96 MHz (28.1 MB/s) than at 120 MHz (21.8 MB/s).

Divider 1 works up to 133 MHz, and even at 150 MHz. That is inside the APS6404's 133 MHz rating but outside the SDK's own rule, which never uses divider 1 above 100 MHz. PicoBench test 513 checked every setting with a 256 KB write/read pattern:

The M33 and Hazard3 agree to within 4 cycles. A long PSRAM read burst does not hold the controller for its whole max-select window: the flash request waits at most ~70 cycles. Streaming collapses in both directions:

  • Cached flash streaming slows ~30x while a PSRAM copy runs (2446 to 77 422 cycles per 4 KB).
  • Uncached PSRAM streaming slows 6.1x while a DMA reads flash through the uncached alias (22 479 to 138 125 cycles per 4 KB).

The earlier "PSRAM loses only ~1 %" was wrong. That DMA read the cached flash alias, so most of its reads were cache hits that never reached the flash controller, and the result moved with the code layout (2 % in one build, 20 % in the next).

Scaling with the system clock

The flash clock divider is fixed (RP2040: 2, RP2350: 3), so the flash SCK scales with clk_sys. On the RP2040 at 200 MHz that means SCK 100 MHz, which PicoBench verifies before keeping it. Every latency in this document is therefore the same number of cycles at every operating point, and its time in nanoseconds scales as 1/f. The one exception is PSRAM, whose divider the SDK changes at 120 MHz (see above).

Overclocking (outside specification)

The overclocking firmware (PICOBENCH_VARIANT=overclock, dist/*_overclock.uf2) raises clk_sys in 10 MHz steps at each core voltage from 1.10 to 1.30 V. 1.30 V is the regulator's limit while it is locked. Each step lasts 250 ms:

  • both cores run a checked integer kernel;
  • core 0 also runs the ARM interpreter on a synthetic load, SRAM and DMA patterns, a flash read-back and a PSRAM pattern.

During the search the flash stays at SCK 75 MHz or less and PSRAM at 133 MHz or less, so only the microcontroller runs outside its rating (the flash probe below goes up to 133 MHz). The last good step is then run again for 1.5 s. A hang is caught by the watchdog, and the run goes on with the next voltage. Four runs (results_10 to results_13) are combined below. Values are the highest step passed in MHz. ✓ = confirmed for 1.5 s, H = the next step hung, so the value is not confirmed.

Conclusions

  1. The two RP2350 boards are the same chip in cycles. All differences come from the flash read mode the boot ROM picks: dual on the black clone, whose QE bit is clear, and quad on the WeAct. With QE set (volatile is enough), the black clone reads quad exactly like the WeAct.
  2. A flash cache miss costs one SPI frame (frame SCK cycles x divider + ~5). The best setting tested on the RP2350 is quad with continuous read at SCK 75 MHz: 0.41 us per miss and 17.7 MB/s on both boards. That matches the RP2040's 0.42 us. The boot defaults are 1.15 us (black clone) and 0.75 us (WeAct).
  3. For hard real-time loops, SRAM decides. An SRAM-resident hot path takes 120-133 ns on the RP2350 and 264 ns on the RP2040 at their default clocks. It is unaffected by flash mode and by DMA in SRAM or PSRAM. A 10 kHz interrupt adds ~250 cycles to its p99.9. Bulk SRAM reads do feel a DMA copy in the same banks: +36 % on the RP2040, +4 % on the RP2350, and nothing when the RP2350's DMA works in the other bank group.
  4. Hazard3 vs M33: Hazard3 is faster for ALU work, copies, the hot path (18 vs 20 cycles) and interpretation (24.4 vs 28.2 cycles per instruction). The M33 is faster for dependent loads and division.
  5. PSRAM is as fast as SRAM when cached and ~0.37 us per uncached access. For random reads over a working set much larger than the 16 KB cache, the uncached alias is 21 % faster than the cached one (53 vs 67 cycles per load). PSRAM DMA can stretch a flash miss to 1.85 us and slow cached flash streaming ~30x. A DMA reading flash slows uncached PSRAM streaming 6x. Keep time-critical code and data out of flash and PSRAM while the other one is busy.
  6. Clock: every latency in cycles is constant from 48 to 200 MHz, so time scales 1/f. The exception is PSRAM, which is faster per access at 96 MHz than at 120-133 MHz because of the SDK's divider choice.
  7. For memory, 133 MHz beats 150 MHz. On the RP2350, divider 1 at 133 MHz gives the fastest flash miss (0.26 us) and the fastest in-spec PSRAM read (0.25 us). The CPU is 11 % slower than at 150 MHz. The PSRAM is rated for 133 MHz. The flash read correctly at SCK 133 MHz on both boards, but its rated maximum was not checked. The RP2040 at 200 MHz (1.15 V) runs everything 1.6x faster than at 125 MHz, with flash SCK 100 MHz.
  8. Overclocking roughly doubles to triples the CPU clock on the RP2040 and on the WeAct RP2350B. It still does not bring the ARM interpreter to the speed most of the games need: at best 4 of 7, on Hazard3 at 420 MHz. A flash miss gains little from it, 0.23 us at best, because the flash clock stays at 133 MHz or less.

Caveats

  • One board of each kind (two RP2040 boards for the last runs), at room temperature. Each flash setting was verified on 2 MB of reads, each PSRAM setting on 256 KB patterns. That is not a long-term stability proof, least of all for the settings with a single working RX delay. The flash chips' rated maximum clocks were not checked against datasheets.
  • Quad, continuous-read and divider-1 settings on the RP2350 were tried by PicoBench tests 431-433 and restored afterwards. They are not what the boards use by default.
  • Until 2026-11-07 the flash and PSRAM sampling loops ran from flash. Their instruction fetches shared the XIP port with the access being measured. Cache hits read 12 / 8 / 8 cycles instead of 10 / 4 / 4, and single uncached reads 4 cycles too long. Older reports carry those figures.
  • The RP2350A's internal temperature sensor reads implausible values (-8 to -13 °C), probably a different ADC reference on that board. It does not affect any timing.
  • Numbers come from PicoBench's REPORT.TXT / RESULTS.CSV of the runs listed under "Data"; those files stay on the boards' USB disks and are not part of this repository.
